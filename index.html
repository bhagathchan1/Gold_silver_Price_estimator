Two ways to understand it

1. The simple picture

You pay for four things: the metal itself (gold or silver), a little extra gold the jeweller loses while crafting (wastage), the craftsman's work (making charge), and tax.

Karat is gold purity out of 24 parts. 22K means 22 parts gold and 2 parts copper or silver, so it is harder and better for jewellery. 24K is nearly pure but too soft to wear.

Silver purity is given as fineness per 1,000. 999 is fine silver, 925 is sterling (92.5% silver, the usual jewellery grade), 800 is common for utensils and coins. Silver costs far less per gram, so making charge is a bigger share of the bill.

Why Hyderabad, Karimnagar and Chennai differ: the base price comes from international gold or silver and the dollar-rupee rate, so it is the same everywhere. Local differences come from transport, local demand and jeweller margins, and they are small.

2. From first principles

Metal is priced by mass of pure metal. Purity f (a fraction) scales the pure rate P:

P₍f₎ = P × f    gold: f = K ÷ 24  |  silver: f = fineness ÷ 999

Check: 12,458 × 22 ÷ 24 ≈ 11,420 for 22K gold. For silver, 925 sterling is 92.5% pure, so at ₹165 per gram of fine silver it costs 165 × 925 ÷ 999 ≈ ₹153 per gram.

G = w × P₍f₎    (metal value)
W = G × a ÷ 100    (wastage)
M = G × m ÷ 100    (making)
T = 0.03 × (G + W) + 0.05 × M    (GST)
Price = G + W + M + T

Here w is weight in grams, a is wastage %, m is making %. GST is 3% on the metal part and 5% on the job-work part. Because every term is proportional to G (T is a fixed mix of G + W and M), the price is linear in weight and in the 24K rate, so doubling either doubles the total. The effective cost per gram of metal content is Price ÷ w.

Simplified model. Some jewellers charge making per gram instead of per cent, add hallmarking fees, or apply 3% GST to the whole bill. Always compare against the invoice.
